"Good margin" depends enormously on category, but sellers rarely have a number to compare
against — so here's one, with the reasoning behind it.

## The general benchmark

| Margin | What it means |
|---|---|
| Under 20% | Fragile. One return, lost package, or materials price jump wipes out profit on several sales. |
| 20–30% | Workable but tight. Fine for high-volume, low-touch products; risky for one-person shops with real labor time. |
| 30–50% | The healthy range most sustainable handmade and craft shops land in. |
| 50%+ | Common for digital downloads and low-material-cost items where labor is the main cost, not materials. |

## Why margin, not markup

Margin is profit divided by your **selling price**. Markup is profit divided by your **cost**.
They sound similar and are constantly confused, and mixing them up leads to underpricing.

A $10-cost item sold at $15 has a $5 profit: that's a 50% markup on cost, but only a 33% margin
on price. If you're mentally targeting "50% margin" but actually calculating a 50% markup, you're
pricing about 17 percentage points too low.

## Margin varies a lot by product type

- **Handmade physical goods**: typically 30–45% once materials, packaging and your time are
  costed in honestly.
- **Print-on-demand**: often thinner, 15–30%, because the POD provider takes their own cut
  before Etsy's fees apply. See our
  [POD profit guide](/blog/etsy-print-on-demand-profit-guide/) for real numbers.
- **Digital downloads**: highest margins, frequently 60–85%, since there's no material or
  shipping cost — Etsy's fees become the main cost. Detail in our
  [digital download pricing guide](/blog/etsy-digital-download-pricing/).

## The margin trap: forgetting your own time

The most common margin miscalculation isn't a fee — it's leaving labor out of "materials cost."
A candle that costs $4 in wax and wick but takes 40 minutes to make, pour, and package isn't a
$4-cost product. Price a reasonable hourly rate into the cost side before calculating margin, or
the number you get is measuring something other than whether the business works.

## When a "low" margin is actually fine

A 15% margin on a $200 order is often better cash-in-hand than a 50% margin on a $12 order, once
you account for the fixed per-order costs (listing fee, payment-processing flat fee, packaging
time) that eat proportionally more of small orders. Margin percentage matters, but so does
absolute profit per hour of your time — check both before deciding a price is "too low."
